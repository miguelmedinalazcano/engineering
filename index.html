02 · Project 01 · IFS-08 steel spaceframe · Season 2025/26

Stiffness per kilogram, one tube at a time.

The frame carries every load on the car. The question was not how light it could be, but which tubes earn their mass.

My role · Mechanical Technical Director

I set the design, manufacturing and assembly standards for the season and led the design across chassis, suspension, aero, powertrain and HV/LV integration. The Chassis & Structural team did the detailed CAD and FEA.

The physics we started from

Torsion

K = T / θ

Torsion defines the frame

A torque is applied between the front and rear suspension pickups and the twist between them is measured. A soft frame acts as an extra spring in series with the suspension. It takes over part of the load transfer that the springs and anti-roll bars are meant to control.

Load paths

k = EA / L

Triangles carry load axially

In a triangulated frame each tube works mostly in tension or compression. A tube that does not sit on a load path between pickups adds mass and almost no stiffness.

Yaw

α = Mz / Iz,  I = Σmr²

Mass far out costs the most

With the same yaw moment from the tyres, lower yaw inertia means the car rotates faster into a corner. Inertia grows with the square of the distance, so the outermost mass costs the most.

Energy

E = ½mv²

Lighter car, cooler brakes

Two seasons earlier the team had a hub failure caused by heat from the brakes. Every kilogram removed is energy the brakes no longer have to dissipate at each stop.

What we did

  1. We arranged the frame around the suspension hardpoints and the main load paths.
  2. We compared many configurations, removing or moving tubes that added little torsional stiffness. That meant redundant intermediate tubes where nodes sat close together in the side and rear, and front members we no longer needed after the steering layout changed.
  3. We removed the X-brace in the front bulkhead, because the simulation showed it added no significant stiffness.
  4. We added one diagonal in the lower front floor, near the anti-submarine structure, because the simulation showed a clear gain in torsional stiffness. Deleting is not the goal. Stiffness per kilogram is.
  5. We lowered both hoops and narrowed the frame. A two-level layout keeps the accumulator (TSAC) and the driver as low as possible.

03 · Project 02 · Welding jig and suspension mounts · Gestamp Toledo

Design the process, not just the part.

A frame is only as accurate as the jig it is welded in. For the IFS-08 we designed the jig and every mount around how they would be cut, held and welded.

My role · Mechanical Technical Director

I led the manufacturing plan with Gestamp and set the standards for the jig and the mounts. The Chassis & Structural team designed and built them.

2A · The welding jig

Put every tube where the CAD says it is.

IFS-07

Plywood profiles on a steel frame. Tubes moved during welding and had to be repositioned, and the dimensional errors carried into the suspension points.

IFS-08

Designed directly around the frame CAD. Supports and brackets were made before the tubes arrived, so welding was more precise and repeatable.

  1. Base: two long horizontal profiles, with a set of vertical profiles fixed to them.
  2. We worked out how many vertical profiles we needed and where, so that every tube could be reached from one, with sensible spacing between supports.
  3. We placed the frame and the jig in one SolidWorks assembly and checked every tube: supports evenly spread, no interferences, and a jig that can still be assembled.
  4. Each holding plate is a flat profile shaped to its own tube, so it can be cut and bolted on before the first weld.

Two jig plates as flat profiles: a slot to adjust the height, and a cut-out for the tube

2B · Suspension mounts

From a tube to two flat plates.

  1. The mounts changed from a tube to two parallel plates, so each one can be shaped to the tube it is welded to.
  2. We first wanted a base joining the two plates to keep them parallel. The plates are water-jet cut, which produces flat profiles, so we dropped the base. The bolts through the rod ends hold the plates parallel while they are welded.
  3. The new suspension geometry put two pickups close together (tie rod and rear lower wishbone). We merged them into one three-plate mount. The third plate joins the other two where no tube was close enough, and keeps them parallel.
  4. Rocker and damper mounts: each plate is parallel to the rocker’s plane of rotation and cut to meet its tube at a different angle. Left and right are mirror images.
  5. Where a plate had spare material, topology optimisation showed where to remove it.
Check on the real car: with the frame welded, the front rocker and damper were cycled by hand through their travel before the car first ran.

04 · Project 03 · BSc thesis · Formula Student monocoque

Design and simulation of a CFRP monocoque.

Along the fibre, carbon/epoxy is about three times stiffer per kilogram than steel (E/ρ ≈ 84 vs 27 MJ/kg), but only along the fibre. That makes the laminate the real design problem. I let the rule load cases decide where material goes, then made the result manufacturable. Altair HyperMesh with the OptiStruct solver.

Grade · my own work

10/10

Supervisor: Ángel Rubio López · Read it in the Comillas repository ↗

Requirement

Lighter, without losing stiffness.

The IFS-07 ran a steel tubular spaceframe of about 38.5 kg with a torsional stiffness of around 2,300 Nm/deg. For four years, final-degree projects had worked towards a monocoque for the team. This one set out to finish the job. The objectives:

  • Design a monocoque that meets the Formula Student rules.
  • Make the chassis lighter while keeping or gaining torsional stiffness.
  • Leave the team a working method for composite optimisation.

Constraints · SolidWorks

A surface built around the rules

The cockpit and internal cross-section templates, the Percy driver template and the hoop positions set the minimum geometry. I kept the IFS-07 suspension points and checked them in Optimum Kinematics, so the existing sizing calculations stayed valid.

Model · HyperMesh

Mesh, materials and load paths

  • Shell mesh with a 5 mm target element size.
  • Suspension links modelled as 1D bars, with loads carried into the tub through RBE2 elements.
  • Sandwich laminate: Gurit carbon plies (IMC unidirectional, RC200T 0/90 and XC411 ±45 cloth) over a Dura-Core II 5052 aluminium honeycomb core.

Method

Free-size, size, then shape the plies

For every load step (main hoop, front hoop, TSAC, harness, suspension and impact), free-size optimisation minimises compliance under a volume-fraction constraint. Sizing optimisation then minimises mass while keeping the Hill failure index below 1. I then redrew the resulting ply shapes as balanced plies that can actually be manufactured, before the final analyses.

Decision

Let the loads draw the laminate

The free-size results show where each load case needs material. The thickness contours become ply sets, which I redrew as seven ply shapes.

Iteration

Seven laminates to the final one

The first monocoque laminate weighed 56.88 kg at 2,742.66 Nm/deg. I iterated ply order, honeycomb thickness and the material of each ply. Laminate 007, the final one, comes in at 19.78 kg and 4,089 Nm/deg, with 31 plies in 7 shapes.

Check

Every rule load case, then torsion

To validate it, I applied every rule load case to the final laminate. All of them stayed within the Hill failure criterion. A final torsion case then gave the stiffness result:

  • Main hoop
  • Accumulator container (TSAC) mounting
  • Front and side impact
  • Harness and anti-submarine

Result

31 plies, 7 shapes, 19.78 kg

The final layup across the monocoque. I took manufacturability into account when defining the ply shapes. A full manufacturing study is the next step for the team.

Result

Two honest baselines

Against the first monocoque laminate, optimisation gave −65% mass and +49% stiffness. Against the IFS-07 steel spaceframe the team actually raced, the final monocoque is −48.7% mass and +77.7% stiffness.

05 · Fundamentals in code

Tools I wrote from the equations.

When a question comes up again and again, I write the physics down once and let the computer do the repetitive part.

Managing Composites · Python

Laminate search and optimisation

The tool screens thousands of candidate laminates against a baseline. Engineers get lighter layups that keep the bending, torsional and strength properties they need. It is built from classical laminate theory, with no black box:

Q̄(θ)→[A B D]→ε⁰, κ→ply stresses→Tsai-Wu
Theory
Classical laminate theory (ABD matrices), first-ply failure with Tsai-Wu and Tsai-Hill strength ratios over 6 load cases
Solves for
Ply count, material selection, ply angles, symmetry and balance, and mixed materials between plies
Two modes
Optimisation near a baseline laminate, or search by target stiffness
Output
Ranked candidates on areal weight against D11, D22, D33 and the minimum strength ratio, with a “best balanced” pick
Stack
Python, multi-core search, web interface, CSV material library

Try the core of it

Why ply angle matters

135GPaEx at this angle
×1.0Material for the same stiffness

Off-axis stiffness of one unidirectional carbon/epoxy ply: 1/Ex = cos⁴θ/E₁ + (1/G₁₂ − 2ν₁₂/E₁)·sin²θ·cos²θ + sin⁴θ/E₂, with typical values E₁ = 135, E₂ = 10, G₁₂ = 5 GPa and ν₁₂ = 0.3. Turn a ply 15° and it loses more than half its stiffness. The full tool runs this for every ply and every candidate laminate.

Shown at 0°: Ex = 135 GPa. At 45°: about 13 GPa.
